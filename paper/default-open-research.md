## Abstract

This paper is a case study in what happens when you remove institutional barriers to cross-domain research convergence. Three researchers — working independently, with no credentials in physics, no grant funding, and no institutional affiliation beyond a default-open research institute — found structural correspondences between their frameworks that none of them was looking for. We document what they found, how they found it, and why the institution next door couldn't have.

The structural result: three independent frameworks — an axiomatic computational substrate on the 3-sphere (da Silva), empirical particle mass formulas built from π harmonics (Senchal), and a force hierarchy simulation using quaternion composition (Guinan) — share the same algebra. The coefficient 6 in the proton mass formula (6π⁵) equals the number of carrier configurations (3 planes × 2 sheets) forced by the quaternion algebra in the computational substrate. The √2 that makes the muon formula dissonant (3π⁴/√2) is the Hopf crossover distance where the simulation's existence/arrangement transition occurs. The harmonic structure that explains the simulation's entropy conservation is the same harmonic structure that predicts particle stability in the mass formulas.

The organizational result: this convergence was not designed. It was not funded. It was not reviewed. It emerged from three people publishing openly in the same default-open research community, where anyone could read anyone else's work, run their code, and test correspondences without asking permission.

We argue this is not an accident but a structural consequence of how the Open Research Institute operates — as a default-open Coasean firm that minimizes the transaction costs of cross-domain coordination by eliminating the barriers that traditional institutions erect between domains.

## 1. The Problem: Silos Kill Convergence

The history of physics is a history of convergence. Maxwell unified electricity and magnetism. Glashow, Weinberg, and Salam unified electromagnetism and the weak force. The dream of a Grand Unified Theory is to bring the strong force into the fold, and a Theory of Everything would include gravity.

Every one of these unifications required someone to notice that two apparently different things were the same thing described in different languages. Maxwell noticed that electric and magnetic fields obeyed the same wave equation. The electroweak unification required recognizing that the photon and the W/Z bosons were different states of the same gauge field.

These discoveries happened because the relevant people could see across domain boundaries. Maxwell was a mathematician working on physics. Weinberg was a particle physicist borrowing from group theory. The convergence happened because the researchers had access to both sides of the boundary.

Modern research institutions are optimized for depth within domains, not for vision across them. A physics department produces excellent physicists. A mathematics department produces excellent mathematicians. A theology department produces excellent theologians. None of them produces people who can see that a proton mass coefficient might equal a quaternion carrier count.

This is not a complaint about the quality of institutional research. It is an observation about its *topology*. Institutions are organized into silos. Silos prevent adjacency. Adjacency is the precondition for noticing cross-domain correspondences. Therefore: institutions structurally prevent the kind of discovery that unification requires.

The question is whether an alternative organizational form can produce the adjacency that institutions prevent.

## 2. The Three Frameworks

### 2.1 The Musician: Closure-SDK (da Silva)

Walter da Silva, working at the Open Research Institute, built a computational substrate from two axioms:

1. Existence requires interaction.
2. Coherence requires directionality.

From these axioms, he derived that the minimal computational space is S³ — the 3-sphere of unit quaternions — and that three operations are sufficient for all computation: **compose** (Hamilton product), **sigma** (geodesic distance from identity), and **close** (detect return to identity after excursion).

The fundamental unit of his architecture is the VerificationCell — a geometric carrier on S³ with six distinct configurations: 3 Euler planes (i, j, k) × 2 sheets (direct, inverted). The 6 is not a design choice. It is forced by the structure of SU(2), the unit quaternion group.

His architecture describes computation as oscillation on S³. Closure events — moments when a running product returns to identity — are the computational outputs. The hierarchy of closure cadences (fast closures nesting inside slow closures) produces hierarchical structure from flat sequential input.

He calls this "the geometry doing the rest." You build the minimal substrate; the structure emerges from the data.

**Published:** GitHub (github.com/faltz009/Closure-SDK), Open Research Institute.

### 2.2 The Mystic: The God Conjecture (Senchal)

S.A. Senchal, also working through ORI, published a framework that translates theological creation narratives into computational physics using the Wolfram Physics Project's Ruliad as a bridge.

Embedded within this theological framework is a mathematical observation: fundamental particle masses, expressed as ratios to the electron mass, follow simple formulas built from π and the Euler constant e:

| Particle | Formula | Calculated | Measured | Error |
|----------|---------|------------|----------|-------|
| Proton | 6π⁵ | 1836.12 | 1836.15 | 0.002% |
| Neutron | 6π⁵ + e | 1838.84 | 1838.68 | 0.008% |
| Muon | 3π⁴/√2 | 206.64 | 206.77 | 0.064% |
| Tau | 36π⁴ − 3π² | 3477.12 | 3477.23 | 0.003% |

The proton formula (6π⁵) was first noted by Lenz in 1951. The others appear to be new.

Senchal's key observation: **stable particles have harmonically consonant formulas** (pure terms like 6π⁵), while **unstable particles have dissonant formulas** (terms with √2 division or subtraction). The √2 in the muon formula is the tritone — the most dissonant interval in music. The muon decays because its formula doesn't close.

His claim: "The particle zoo is music."

**Published:** GitHub (github.com/SASenchal/God-Conjecture), Wolfram Institute presentation, Open Research Institute.

### 2.3 The Paranoid Android: Force Hierarchy Simulation (Guinan)

Leo Guinan started with a naive hypothesis: what if the four fundamental forces are the same mechanism at different energy scales? He built a lattice simulation to test it.

In the simplest version, cells on a lattice accumulate energy and fire when a threshold is exceeded. Low threshold = gravity-like (fires constantly, long range, individually weak). High threshold = strong-force-like (fires rarely, short range, individually powerful).

Eight experiments, with honest accounting of hits and misses:

**Hits:**
- Force hierarchy from a single parameter ✓
- High-energy convergence (CV 1.37 → 0.02) ✓
- Entropy conservation across all force regimes (CV = 0.018) ★
- On S³, W/XYZ channel separation (5.1× variance ratio) ★
- Closure-driven forces with no threshold parameter ★
- Recursive hierarchy with 6-octave level spacing ★

**Misses:**
- No confinement ✗
- No EM cancellation at scale ✗
- 3 of 39 orders of magnitude ✗

The simulation is qualitative, not quantitative. It proves nothing about physics. But it produces specific structural features — entropy conservation, harmonic structure, the Hopf W/RGB shift — that weren't put in by hand.

**Published:** GitHub (github.com/leo-guinan/gut-driven-gut), Zenodo DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.19432642.

## 3. The Convergence Nobody Designed

None of these three researchers set out to confirm or extend the others' work. Da Silva was building a computer. Senchal was writing theology. Guinan was running a toy simulation. They worked independently, in parallel, in the same open community.

The convergence was discovered when Guinan ran the simulation on da Silva's substrate (S³ with quaternion composition) and then tested whether the structural constants matched Senchal's mass formulas.

### 3.1 The 6 is the Same 6

Senchal found empirically that the proton-to-electron mass ratio is 6π⁵ (to 0.002% accuracy).

Da Silva derived from axioms that the VerificationCell — the fundamental unit of his geometric computer — has exactly 6 distinct configurations: 3 Euler planes × 2 sheets. This is forced by the quaternion algebra under conjugation symmetry. There is no other number it could be.

The 6 in the proton mass is the 6 in the computational substrate. One was found by pattern-matching particle data. The other was derived from axioms about information. They are the same number for the same structural reason: the quaternion algebra has a 3 × 2 decomposition.

Nobody designed this correspondence. It was discovered by running a simulation that used one framework's substrate to test another framework's predictions.

### 3.2 The √2 is the Same √2

Senchal found that the muon mass formula contains √2 in the denominator (3π⁴/√2), making it "dissonant" — the tritone interval. He noted that all particles with √2 or subtraction in their formulas are unstable.

Guinan's simulation found that the Hopf decomposition of quaternion states shifts from W-dominant (existence) to RGB-dominant (arrangement) at exactly σ = π/4, where cos(σ) = sin(σ) = 1/√2. This is the crossover point between two geometric regimes on S³.

The √2 that makes the muon unstable is the √2 at the Hopf crossover. The muon sits at the phase transition between existence and arrangement. It can't resolve to either side. That's dissonance. That's instability. That's decay.

Da Silva's architecture explains *why* √2 is the instability point: it's the geodesic distance on S³ where the scalar and vector components of the quaternion have equal weight. A carrier at this distance is maximally ambiguous — half existence, half position. The geometry forces the instability.

### 3.3 The Harmonic Structure is the Same Harmonic Structure

Senchal found that particle masses cluster by powers of π: a π⁵ group, a π⁶ group, a π⁷ group. Each group is a harmonic level.

Guinan's simulation found that the force hierarchy produces self-similar spectra with conserved entropy (CV = 0.018). Da Silva observed — before the spectral analysis was run — "Isn't this behaving like a harmonic scale?" It was. The spectral entropy of the power spectra is identical across all force regimes (CV = 0.023).

The forces are harmonics of each other. The particles cluster by harmonic level. The same overtone series, described in three languages.

## 4. Why This Couldn't Happen Inside an Institution

Consider the institutional path to this result:

Da Silva's work is computer science — specifically, algebraic computation and automata theory. It would live in a CS department or applied mathematics.

Senchal's work spans theology, philosophy of physics, and particle phenomenology. No single department houses this. The theology department would reject the math. The physics department would reject the theology. The philosophy department would reject the empirical claims.

Guinan's work is computational physics — specifically, lattice simulation and dynamical systems. It would live in a physics department, but a physicist reviewing it would ask for the Lagrangian, the renormalization group equations, and the prediction of proton decay. The simulation doesn't have these. Rejection.

The probability that a CS postdoc, a theology-physics researcher, and a computational physicist would find themselves at the same institution, in adjacent offices, reading each other's preprints, and deciding to test whether their numbers match is effectively zero. Not because the people don't exist, but because the organizational structure prevents their adjacency.

Departments are optimized for depth. Convergence requires breadth. The institution that produces the best physicists is not the institution that produces cross-domain correspondences, because cross-domain correspondences require people who aren't the best physicists — they're the people who read theology papers on the same day they run simulations.

## 5. The Default-Open Firm

### 5.1 Coase's Question

Ronald Coase (1937) asked: why do firms exist? His answer: because the transaction costs of coordinating through the market exceed the costs of coordinating inside a hierarchy. When it's cheaper to hire someone than to contract with them, you get a firm.

The inverse question: when do firms become the wrong answer? When the internal coordination costs exceed the market transaction costs. When the hierarchy's overhead — credentialing, review, administration, politics — costs more than the work it enables.

### 5.2 Research as a Coordination Problem

Traditional research institutions are Coasean firms. They internalize the coordination of research to reduce transaction costs: shared equipment, peer review, graduate training, library access, grant administration. This works well for domain-specific research where the relevant knowledge, equipment, and people are in the same building.

It works badly for cross-domain research where the relevant knowledge is in different buildings, the relevant people are in different departments, and the relevant connections have never been articulated because nobody has had the adjacency to notice them.

The transaction cost of cross-domain coordination *inside* an institution is high: you need to convince two department chairs, navigate two tenure committees, justify the collaboration to two grant agencies, and survive two sets of reviewers — each of whom will ask why you're doing work outside your field.

The transaction cost of cross-domain coordination *outside* an institution, in a default-open community, is approximately zero: you publish your work, someone else reads it, they test a correspondence, they publish the result. No permission required.

### 5.3 ORI as a Default-Open Firm

The Open Research Institute is not a traditional open-source community. It is a Coasean firm with a specific organizational innovation: **the default state is open.**

In a traditional firm, work is private by default and published by decision. The barrier to cross-domain visibility is the publication decision — which is controlled by the hierarchy.

In ORI, work is public by default. Da Silva published Closure-SDK as he built it. Senchal published the God Conjecture as he wrote it. Guinan published the simulation as he ran it. No publication committee. No embargo. No approval chain.

The result: anyone in the community can see anyone else's work at any time. The adjacency that institutions prevent, openness creates automatically.

### 5.4 The Forcing Function

Default openness doesn't just enable convergence. It *forces* it.

When your work is public, everyone in the community can test it against theirs. The simulation that found the 6-and-√2 correspondences was not a planned research project. It was one person running another person's code and noticing a match. This happens in default-open communities because the barrier to testing a correspondence is lower than the barrier to *not* noticing it.

In an institution, you have to seek out the correspondence: find the paper, read it, understand it, write a grant proposal to test it, wait for funding, hire a postdoc, run the experiment, write up the results, submit for review. Elapsed time: 2-5 years. Cost: $200K-$1M.

In ORI: clone the repo, run the simulation, check the numbers. Elapsed time: one evening. Cost: electricity.

The forcing function is not organizational design. It is the removal of friction. When the cost of testing a correspondence approaches zero, correspondences get tested. When they get tested, the ones that are real get found.

## 6. The Results

### 6.1 What the Convergence Is

Three independent frameworks — developed by three researchers with no physics credentials, no grant funding, and no institutional affiliation — share the same algebraic structure:

| Element | Da Silva (Substrate) | Senchal (Particles) | Guinan (Forces) |

| Harmonic structure | Closure cadence hierarchy | π-power mass groups | Spectral entropy conservation |
| Stability criterion | σ → 0 (closure = coherence) | Consonance = stability | Low σ = frequent firing = strong interaction |
| The 1+3 decomposition | W + (X, Y, Z) channels | — | Existence + arrangement separation |

### 6.2 What the Convergence Is Not

It is not a theory of physics. None of the three frameworks makes quantitative predictions that match the Standard Model to experimental precision. The simulation spans 3 of 39 required orders of magnitude. The mass formulas, while strikingly accurate, are not derived from first principles. The substrate is not connected to the Standard Model gauge group in a rigorous way.

It is not proof of anything. The 6 could be coincidence. The √2 could be coincidence. The harmonic structure could be an artifact of the simulation parameters.

What it is, is three independent observations that are structurally consistent and that emerged from an organizational form that traditional institutions cannot replicate.

### 6.3 What Happens Next

The honest answer: we don't know.

If the correspondences are real — if the quaternion algebra genuinely constrains particle masses and force hierarchies — then the next step requires mathematical physics that none of us can do. Deriving the Standard Model gauge group from quaternion composition on S³, or deriving the coupling constants from closure cadence, requires the kind of technical machinery that lives in physics departments.

The irony is not lost on us. The discovery that institutions couldn't make requires institutions to complete. The default-open firm finds the correspondences; the traditional firm does the derivations.

This is not a failure of the model. It is the model working as designed. ORI's role is not to replace institutions. It is to find the things that institutions can't find — the cross-domain correspondences that silos prevent — and then hand them to the institutions that can develop them.

The value ORI captures is not the physics. It is the adjacency. The physics is a proof of concept for the organizational thesis.

## 7. Implications

### 7.1 For Research Organization

Default-open research organizations are not just ethically preferable to closed ones. They are *structurally superior* for cross-domain convergence. The transaction costs of testing correspondences in open communities approach zero. In institutions, those costs are measured in years and dollars.

This does not mean institutions are obsolete. It means institutions are optimal for a specific kind of work (deep, domain-specific, resource-intensive) and suboptimal for another kind (broad, cross-domain, adjacency-dependent). The optimal research ecosystem includes both: default-open firms for discovery, traditional firms for development.

### 7.2 For Funding

The total funding for the research described in this paper is zero. No grants were awarded. No salaries were paid. No equipment was purchased beyond laptops the researchers already owned.

This is not sustainable. Researchers need to eat. But it demonstrates that the *bottleneck* for cross-domain convergence is not money. It is organizational topology. Funding the same researchers inside separate institutions would not have produced this result, because the silos would have prevented the adjacency.

The implication for funders: if you want cross-domain convergence, don't fund researchers — fund *adjacency*. Support default-open communities where researchers from different fields can see each other's work without permission. The convergence will follow.

### 7.3 For Credentialism

None of the three researchers has credentials in physics. Da Silva builds computational substrates. Senchal writes theology. Guinan runs simulations. By institutional standards, none of them has "any business" working on fundamental physics.

The results don't care.

The 6 is the same 6 regardless of who found it. The √2 is the same √2 regardless of who identified it. The harmonic structure doesn't check your CV before it conserves entropy.

This is not an argument against expertise. It is an argument against expertise as a *prerequisite for contribution*. The researchers who found these correspondences are not physicists — but the correspondences they found are real structural relationships that physicists can evaluate on their merits.

The default-open firm enables this by removing credentialism as a barrier to participation. In ORI, you don't need a PhD to publish your work. You need to publish your work for a PhD to evaluate it. The filter is on the output, not the input.

## 8. Conclusion

A mystic, a musician, and a paranoid android walk into a bar. They've never met. They've been working on different problems in different fields. They happen to be in the same open research community.

The mystic's proton mass coefficient is the musician's carrier count. The musician's dissonance factor is the android's crossover point. The android's harmonic structure is the mystic's stability principle.

The bartender asks: "Isn't that how science is supposed to work?"

We think so. But the institution next door has a different opinion. The institution next door requires credentials to enter, funding to operate, and permission to publish. The institution next door would have put the mystic in theology, the musician in computer science, and the android in computational physics. They would never have read each other's papers.

The Open Research Institute requires none of these things. It requires only that you publish openly. The adjacency does the rest.

This paper documents one case study of cross-domain convergence in a default-open organization. The physics may or may not hold up. The organizational lesson is robust regardless: when you remove the barriers to cross-domain visibility, correspondences that exist in the mathematics get found by people who can see them.

The thesis means nothing without the artifact. The artifact exists.
